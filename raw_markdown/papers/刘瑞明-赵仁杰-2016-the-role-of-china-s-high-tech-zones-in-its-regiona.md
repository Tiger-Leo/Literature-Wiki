# The Role of China’s High-Tech Zones in Its Regional Economic Development

Liu Ruiming (刘瑞明) and Zhao Renjie (赵仁杰)

Research Institute, People’s Bank of China, Beijing, China

Abstract: National high-tech industrial development zones represent a major strategy of the Chinese government in developing high-tech industries and spurring regional economic growth. The effect of this policy is yet to be proven. Based on the panel data of 283 Chinese cities between 1984 and 2012, this paper investigates the effect of high-tech zones on regional economic development. Empirical results suggest that first, national hightech zones have remarkably enhanced regional GDP and per capita GDP growth, and this conclusion still holds true after multiple rounds of robustness test; second, the economic effect of national high-tech zones is subject to diminishing marginal return and compared with higher-tier cities, lower-tier cities benefited more from the creation of national high tech zones. This paper concludes that national high-tech zones where special policies are experimented and offered not only drive economic development but, if properly distributed across regions, may help reduce regional economic disparities as well. The results of this study provide important implications for the future distribution of national high-tech zones and the creation of other relevant policies.

Keywords: national high-tech zones, regional economic growth, difference-in-differences method

JEL Classification Codes: E43, E44, E52, E63

## 1. Introduction

As an important economic policy first enacted in the early 1980s, national hightech industrial development zones (hereinafter “national high-tech zones”) played a pivotal role in the development of technology industries in China, outshining special economic zones (SEZs) and the Pudong Development Zone (Cao, 2004). Since Zhongguancun Science Park, the first national high-tech zone, was unveiled in 1988, China has created a total of 114 national hightech zones, which boosted high-tech industries and economic growth (Lyu Zheng, Zhang Kejun, 2006). According to the estimate of the Torch High Technology Industry Development Center of the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST), the total business revenues of the 114 national high-tech zones amounted to 20.3 trillion yuan in 2013 with value-added reaching 5.8 trillion yuan, accounting for about 10% of China’s GDP. Launched against the backdrop of the new technology revolution, high-tech zones have spearheaded and experimented with innovation-driven development and assumed the major tasks of innovation, R&D and industry upgrade (Jiang Cailou, Xu Kangning, 2009; Cheng Yu, Chen Xue, 2013). National high-tech zones have spawned a multitude of emerging industries as the key drivers of growth in many cities (Li Qiang, Han Botang, 2007).

Despite these achievements, high-tech zones have been restrained by a host of institutional hurdles. For instance, overreliance on policy preferences and infrastructure construction to attract investment coexists with a lack of innovation (Lyu Zheng, Zhang Kejun, 2006). Inefficient land expansion and investment promotion practices led to diseconomies of scale in many high-tech zones since 2001, while factordriven economic growth lost steam (Cheng Yu, Chen Xue, 2013). Inadequate competitiveness, undefined ownership and pervasive government intervention have prevented high-tech zones from upgrading into R&D centers and incubation parks on par with Silicon Valley. Given these problems, this paper attempts to answer the following questions: Have national high-tech zones contributed to regional economic development? If so, in which cities should they be created in order to be more conducive to growth and balanced regional development? The role of national hightech zones in regional economic growth must be accurately evaluated to guide future policy formulation and implementation.

Hence, this paper intends to investigate how high-tech zones contribute to economic growth based on the panel data of Chinese cities at or above the prefecture level (hereinafter “cities” as a general reference) and following the differencein-differences approach. This paper provides the following contributions. First, we employ the data of China’s 283 cities between 1994 and 2012, which not only expanded the sample size and made the conclusions more robust, but allowed more scientific evaluation methods to be utilized to verify the role of national high-tech zones in promoting regional economic growth. Second, following the difference-in-differences approach, this paper has identified the net effect of hightech zones on economic growth and employed multiple methods to carry out a robustness test on the results. Lastly, a detailed analysis on the regional differences regarding such net effect is conducted. With the initial stages of economic development for various regions taken into account, it has been discovered that the economic effect of national high-tech zones is subject to diminishing marginal return. The implication is that national high-tech zones should be regionally distributed in an optimal manner to bring out the potentials of less developed regions and reduce regional disparities.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Part 2 elaborates on the policy background and theoretical hypotheses for the creation of national high-tech zones; Part 3 introduces the identification method, data and variables; Part 4 discusses the empirical test and presents the basic results, the common trend test and the counterfactual test, among others; Part 5 offers the conclusions and policy recommendations.

## 2. Policy Background and Theoretical Hypotheses

## 2.1 Policy Background

Given the hurdles of ideology and policy inertia under the traditional system, China’s gradualist reform must seek breakthrough points and be accomplished incrementally. In addition to establishing Special Economic Zones and “coastal ports” at the inception of reform and opening up in the early 1980s, institutional innovation must be carried out for China’s hinterland as well. The creation of national high-tech zones offered an ideal path to overcome the barriers of the traditional system, experiment with new reforms and boost the development of noncoastal regions. On the other hand, technological progress became a key driver of economic growth. Judging by the development of hightech industries in the mid-to-late 20th century, industrial clusters, technology parks and science towns with a high concentration of tech firms outperformed in new technologies and products (Goetz and Rupasingha, 2002). Thus, it became a reasonable choice for China to create national high-tech zones to bolster economic efficiency and indigenous innovation. In May 1988, Zhongguancun Science Park was approved by the State Council to become China’s first national high-tech zone. In the following two decades, national high-tech zones mushroomed across China. By 2014, a total of 114 national high-tech zones had been created<sup>1</sup>.

## 2.2 Theoretical Hypotheses

In retrospect, we have discovered the following three factors that have led to the rapid emergence of national high-tech zones over the past three decades, contributing to local economic development and transition.

The first factor is special policy preferences granted by local governments to high-tech zones. In 1991, the State Science and Technology Commission and the State Administration of Taxation (SAT) enacted regulations to officially establish policy preferences in such areas as taxation, credit, land and human resources.

(1) Tax credits: A company newly established in a high-tech zone is entitled to exemption from income tax for a period of two years after the date of production. As of the date when a company located in a high-tech zone is identified as a high-tech company, the company is entitled to a preferential income tax rate of 15%; if the value of exported goods exceeds 7% of the total output value of the company, the company is entitled to an additional preferential income tax rate of 10%.

(2) Credit preference: Banks must offer proactive financing support to high-tech zones and issue a certain quota of long-term bonds to them. High-tech zones may apply to establish venture investment companies and with the approval of local governments, may be exempted from the obligation to purchase national key construction bonds.

(3) Preferences of land and human resources: Local governments have formulated preferentia land policies and programs to recruit highly qualified professionals. Under these policy preferences, a great number of companies opened offices and factories in high-tech zones.

Secondly, high-tech zones have served as carriers for high-tech and related industries. It takes hefty investments to construct high-tech zones in the first place, and the entry of foreignfunded companies will bring about a great deal of FDI as well. For instance, the Samsung Semiconductor Project in Xi’an High-Tech Industries Development Zone announced in 2013 involved a Phase I investment of some USD7 billion, which was equivalent to two times the total amount of actual FDI utilization in Shaanxi province in that year<sup>2</sup>. In order to support the Samsung project, the provincial government also invested heavily in infrastructure, thus beefing up related industries. By reducing the cost of transaction among related manufacturers and offering shared infrastructure, industrial agglomeration helps companies become more competitive through greater economies of scale. High-tech zones provide an arena for industrial agglomeration. Measured by the speed, quality and yield of factor agglomeration, national hightech zones have formed an initial pattern of industrial agglomeration with growing returns to scale over the years (Li Qiang, Han Botang, 2007). Furthermore, complete infrastructure and the entry of high-quality companies also provide the catalyst for industrial agglomeration through specialization, knowledge spillover and shared infrastructure. Such industrial agglomeration will boost innovation and spur regional economic growth (Zhu Bin, Wang Yu, 2004).

Furthermore, chemistry from clustering hightech firms undergirds innovation, transforming traditional factor-driven growth into innovationdriven growth (Porter, 2001). In the early stage, national high-tech zones relied on investment promotion and policy preferences. In 2001, the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST) called for a “second-round development” of hightech zones by weaning off policy incentives and moving towards endogenous growth. Empirically, high-tech zones outperformed the average level of local provinces and cities in terms of TFP (total factor productivity) growth, which mainly derives from technological progress. By inducing industrial clustering and regional competition, high-tech zones have transformed the regional market scale, demand and income structure, thus elevating local innovation (Li Kai, et al., 2007; Yan Guoqing, et al., 2008; Chen Jianjun, Hu Chenguang, 2008; Cheng Yu, Chen Xue, 2013). High-tech zones have achieved efficient economic growth spurred by innovation (Cheng Yu, Chen Xue, 2013).

In light of the above analysis, we have put forward the following theoretical hypotheses to be tested.

Hypothesis 1: National high-tech zones can promote regional economic development.

As far as R&D and high-tech industries are concerned, differences in factor endowment across countries and regions will result in drastically different effects of R&D and economic performance (Acemoglu et al., 2006). Initial factor endowments will cause the same economic policy to play out differently. Different regions across China vary greatly in terms of resource endowments, the initial level of economic development and economic policies (Li Xinchun et al., 2004). Compared with less developed regions, developed regions boast much better infrastructure and policy preferences similar to those offered in high-tech zones. Based on the principle of diminishing marginal return, creating high-tech zones in these regions only turns them from good investment destinations into better ones, with smaller marginal effect. However, due to relatively backward infrastructure and complicated policy barriers in less developed regions, creating national high-tech zones in less developed regions will make a much greater difference to the local economy.

In China, great differences also exist in factor endowment among cities. In China’s current political and economic systems, such differences are highly correlated with administrative hierarchies. Even within a province, the levels of economic development, human resources and market scale differ greatly across cities of different tiers. Under China’s hierarchical system of cities, cities are classified into provincial capitals, sub-provincial cities<sup>3</sup>, big cities and cities. Higher-tier cities possess superior factors and development conditions and thus the same policy preferences’ effects are relatively less remarkable there. Therefore, we hypothesize that the marginal effect of national high-tech zones on economic development will diminish with increasing tiers and endowments of cities. Thus, we may put forward:

Hypothesis 2: Based on the pattern of diminishing marginal return, national high-tech zones play a greater role in promoting economic growth of lower-tier cities compared with highertier cities.

## 3. Model, Methodology and Data

## 3.1 Methodology

Among the 283 city samples, by 2012, a total of 105 cities were approved to establish national high-tech zones, which provide us with a good opportunity of quasi-natural experiment to adopt the difference in differences method. Specifically, among our samples, a total of 105 cities were approved to establish national high-tech zones and these cities comprise the treatment group, while the rest cities not approved to establish national high-tech zones naturally comprise the control group. The State Council approved the establishment of national high-tech zones in different groups. Between 1994 and 2012, the State Council approved the establishment of one high-tech zone in 1997, one in 2007, two in 2009, 26 in 2010, five in 2011, and 17 in 2012. According to the time of approval, we have created Hightech variable:Hightech=0 before the approval for the establishment of high-tech zone, and $H i g h t e c h { = } 1$ after such approval. In this manner, we may create the following twoway fixed effect econometric model to realize the difference in differences and test the net effect of high-tech zones on regional economic growth:

$$
\mathrm{Y} _ {\mathrm{it}} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text { Hightech } _ {\mathrm{it}} + \alpha \mathrm{X} _ {\mathrm{it}} + \gamma_ {\mathrm{t}} + \mu_ {\mathrm{i}} + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}} \tag {1}
$$

Where, $Y _ { i t }$ is explained variable and this paper has selected the logarithm of real GDP $\ln g d p _ { i t }$ and the logarithm of per capita GDP $\mathrm { l n } p e r d p _ { i t }$ to measure the effect of high-tech zones on regional economic development. Subscripts i and t respectively denote city i and year t; γ is the fixed effect of time, μi is the individual fixed effect of city. $X _ { i t }$ is other Controled variables, including the growth rate of regional fixed asset investment, the level of FDI, government size, the level of education for regional population, the level of development for regional secondary industry, the level of urbanization and regional total savings rate, etc. For the above model, the estimated value of coefficient $\beta _ { \mathfrak { r } }$ is the focus of our attention as it measures the net effect of high-tech zones on regional economic development. If the economic policy of hightech zones is proven to boost regional growth, the coefficient $\beta _ { \mathfrak { r } }$ should be significantly positive.

## 4.2 Robustness Test

In estimating the effect of national high-tech zone policies using the difference in differences approach, an important assumption is that if the impact of national high-tech zone does not exist, the treatment group and control group must share consistent tendencies of developmen without systematic differences over time. Among our samples, this assumption is brought into question due to the fact that the Chinese government would consider various conditions of cities in approving the establishment of national high-tech zones, giving priority to those cities with better infrastructure, more complete systems and adequate supporting conditions. Therefore, the trends of development are not consistent between cities with and without national high-tech zones. In order to address this problem, we need to test whether systematic differences exist between different cities.

We have observed that under China’s special background, the various conditions among cities are related to the cities’ hierarchy. Higher-tier cities with superior infrastructure and better conditions are more likely to be approved to create national high-tech zones.

Therefore, the hierarchy of city is an appropriate perspective to investigate the common trend hypothesis. Given that our samples are cities, we tested the hypothesis using three administrative hierarchies including provincial capitals, subprovincial cities and “big cities.” Provincial capital is normally the capital of a certain province (autonomous region) and represents the economic, political and cultural center of a region with the highest hierarchy and the best conditions of development. In addition, the subprovincial cities that were formerly “cities under separate planning” also boast greater advantages compared with other cities in terms of the resource factors and policy preferences but are slightly below the hierarchy of provincia capitals. In China, a special hierarchy of administrative region is “big cities”, which have the same authority of local legislation as provincial capitals. Such legislative power can be used to promote regional economic development. In the broad sense, big cities include the cities as capital seats of various provinces and autonomous regions, cities as seats of special economic zones, as well as other big cities approved by the State Council<sup>6</sup>.

The above three types of cities boast much more favorable conditions in terms of factor endowments, economic autonomy and local legislation. Hence, among our samples, these three types of cities are the most likely to violate the common trend hypothesis. If the explained variables are affected by the type of cities, the implication is that systematic differences exist among regions. Otherwise, regional development trends will not have systematic differences due to the tier of cities.

We have presented the results for the three types of cities in Table 4. As can be discovered from the results, the estimated logarithm of regional GDP and the estimated logarithm of regional per capita GDP are highly similar among the three types of cities and, with slight differences of a few values, the estimation results are almost identical. Besides, the indicator for the hierarchy of cities is insignificant for the three types of cities including provincial capitals, sub-provincial capitals and “big cities”, meaning that without the effect of high-tech zones taken into account, there are no systematic differences in the trends of economic development between treatment group and control group. This explains that the results of the difference in differences method in Table 3 are credible.

## 5. Conclusions and Policy Implications

Over the past three decades, national hightech zones mushroomed as the cradles of hightech industries in China. It is highly important to assess the role of high-tech zones in promoting regional economic development. We employ the panel data of 283 cities in China (1994 -2012) and the difference-in-differences method to verify whether high-tech zones have contributed to regional economic growth. Our study suggests that the creation of high-tech zones can significantly increase regional GDP aggregate and per capita GDP level, and this result still holds true after multiple robustness tests. In addition, the contribution of high-tech zones to growth is subject to the law of diminishing marginal return, and high-tech zones play a lesser role in promoting regional economic development for cities of higher hierarchies and greater factor endowments. This discovery shows that if properly distributed regionally, the creation of national high-tech zones can decrease economic disparities between developed and less developed regions in addition to contributing to overall economic development.

In China’s reality, despite progressive reform and opening up policies in all regions, some particular regions still suffer from lackluster growth and lag behind others. As a major policy experiment, national high-tech zones undoubtedly provide an antidote to these conundrums. Given their proven effect on economic growth, the Chinese government should spare no efforts in developing national high-tech zones as policy experiments. Less developed regions, in particular, enjoy greater potentials of development and are bound to benefit more from such a policy. It is recommended that national high-tech zones and other relevant policy experiments be reasonably distributed across regions, giving priority to those lower-tier cities for more balanced regional development.
