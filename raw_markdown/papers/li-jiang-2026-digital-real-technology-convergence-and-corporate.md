# Digital–Real Technology Convergence and Corporate Carbon Performance: An Empirical Analysis of Mechanisms and Boundary Conditions

Jinke Li and Tonghui Jiang \*

College of Business, Qingdao University, Qingdao 266000, China; lijinke@gmail.com \* Correspondence: jiangtonghui@qdu.edu.cn; Tel.: +86-15253541661

## Abstract

Against the backdrop of the accelerating integration of the digital and real economies, exploring how digital—real technology convergence enables corporate decarbonization and green upgrading represents a critical pathway. This development pathway is conducive to promoting the high-quality growth of industry while aligning with China’s strategic goals of carbon peaking and carbon neutrality. Based on panel data from Chinese A-share listed manufacturing enterprises during 2012–2023, and employing a fixed-effects model, this study empirically investigates how DRTC influences firms’ carbon performance, as well as the mechanisms through which this effect is transmitted. In addition, this research explores the intermediary function of green innovation and further investigates the contingent effects of R&D investment and financing constraints. The results show that DRTC contributes significantly to improving corporate carbon performance, with the validity of this conclusion supported by multiple robustness examinations. Green innovation acts as a partial mediator, channeling a portion of DRTC’s carbon-reduction benefits. R&D investment amplifies these positive effects, whereas financing constraints create a notable drag on DRTC’s effectiveness. Heterogeneity analysis adds nuance, showing that DRTC’s positive impact is substantially more pronounced in large-scale firms and asset-intensive enterprises. By clarifying the internal mechanisms and limiting conditions that underlie DRTC’s improvement of corporate carbon performance, this study enriches the interdisciplinary literature by linking research on the digital economy with discussions on green and low-carbon development.

Keywords: digital–real technology convergence; corporate carbon performance; green innovation; R&D investment; financing constraints

## 1. Introduction

Under China’s carbon-peaking and carbon-neutrality goals, balancing sustained economic growth with a low-carbon transition has become a key concern in the pursuit of high-quality development. At the firm level, corporate carbon performance provides an important indicator for evaluating this coordination as it captures the amount of economic value created for each unit of carbon emissions, rather than only the absolute reduction of emissions. Prior research has shown that green R&D and eco-innovation can reduce carbon emissions while improving firm performance, indicating that carbon performance captures both environmental and economic dimensions of corporate sustainability [1]. Evidence from Chinese firms also suggests that digitalization may affect corporate carbon performance through green technological innovation, although this effect may be nonlinear and context-dependent [2]. At the macro level, research on China’s carbon-neutrality pathway further emphasizes that energy-economic transition is essential for balancing economic development and emission reduction [3].

Prior research on firms’ carbon performance has largely examined the roles of policy interventions, financial conditions, corporate governance, and technological determinants. Environmental regulation and carbon emission trading schemes can reshape firms’ cost– benefit structures and encourage cleaner production and energy-saving adjustments [4]. Green finance can alleviate financing pressure and support long-term green investment; for example, corporate green bonds have been found to improve firms’ environmental orientation and sustainability performance [5], while evidence from China shows that green-bond issuance can promote corporate green transformation and generate market incentive effects [6]. Moreover, technological innovation is commonly viewed as a crucial mechanism enabling firms to alleviate environmental pressures and develop competitive advantages in low-carbon markets [7,8].

Recent studies have paid growing attention to the environmental implications associ ated with the adoption of digital technologies. Technologies associated with Industry 4.0, including big data analytics, artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and cyber–physical systems, can improve information processing, production monitoring, resource allocation, and coordination efficiency, thereby generating potential sustainability benefits [9]. At the enterprise level, digital transformation may improve ESG performance by reinforcing firms’ capabilities for green innovation [10]. At the sectoral level, evidence suggests that the digital economy supports the green transformation of manufacturing through gains in production efficiency and the advancement of technological upgrading [11]. Empirical evidence from Chinese manufacturing firms further indicates that digital transformation may influence carbon emissions by fostering green technological innovation [12].

However, most existing studies focus on general digital transformation or regional digital economy development, while the firm-level implications of digital–real technology convergence (DRTC) remain insufficiently examined. This distinction is important because the environmental value of digitalization depends not only on the adoption of isolated digital tools, but also on the integration of data, algorithms, platforms, sensors, intelligent equipment, and physical production systems. Emerging studies suggest that digital–real technology integration can promote corporate green transformation [13], improve energy productivity through artificial intelligence applications and embodied technological progress [14], and stimulate urban green innovation [15]. Nevertheless, the micro-level relationship between DRTC and corporate carbon performance remains underexplored. In particular, the mechanism through which DRTC is translated into carbon performance improvement, as well as the firm-level conditions that strengthen or weaken this effect, require further investigation.

The linkage between DRTC and firms’ carbon performance can be interpreted through the lenses of the resource-based view and dynamic capability theory. According to the resource-based view, enterprises are more likely to achieve enduring competitive advantages when they control resources that are valuable, scarce, difficult to imitate, and effectively embedded within organizational processes [16]. Dynamic capability theory further highlights the capacity of firms to integrate, reshape, and renew both internal and external resources when facing environmental changes [17]. In the context of low-carbon transition, DRTC can be regarded as a strategic resource bundle formed by digital infras tructure, data resources, intelligent algorithms, production equipment, and organizational routines. This resource bundle may help firms identify inefficient energy use, optimize production scheduling, reduce material and energy waste, and improve carbon management coordination. Therefore, DRTC is expected to improve corporate carbon performance by enhancing operational efficiency and supporting low-carbon competitive advantages.

Green innovation may serve as an important mechanism linking DRTC to corporate carbon performance. Green innovation refers to innovation activities that reduce envi ronmental burdens, including cleaner production technologies, energy-saving processes, pollution-control technologies, and environmentally friendly products. It differs from carbon performance because green innovation reflects firms’ low-carbon innovation efforts and capability-building processes, whereas carbon performance captures the realized effi ciency outcome associated with carbon emissions. DRTC can promote green innovation by improving access to production data, strengthening cross-departmental knowledge sharing, and reducing the uncertainty of green R&D. Enabled by DRTC, firms are better positioned to detect energy-intensive production stages, model cleaner production alternatives, and speed up the development of low-carbon technologies and environmentally friendly products. Green innovation may subsequently enhance the efficiency of resource use while lowering the intensity of energy consumption, thereby contributing to better corporate carbon performance [18,19].

The influence of DRTC on firms’ carbon performance may be contingent on both internal investment in innovation and external financing conditions. In this study, R&D investment is adopted as an indicator of the financial resources devoted to firms’ innovation activities. Although R&D activities also depend on human capital and accumulated technological knowledge, financial investment remains a necessary condition for sustaining digital upgrading, green technology development, equipment renewal, and low-carbon process optimization. Firms characterized by greater R&D intensity are generally better able to provide continuous resource support for innovation projects and to absorb, adapt, and implement digital technologies in carbon management processes. According to absorptive capacity theory, prior investment in innovation helps firms recognize, assimilate, and exploit external technological knowledge [20]. Therefore, higher R&D investment may strengthen the positive relationship between DRTC and corporate carbon performance.

Conversely, financing constraints may dampen the positive effect of DRTC on corporate carbon performance. Digital–real integration and green transformation usually require continuous investment in data infrastructure, intelligent equipment, skilled labor, R&D activities, and organizational restructuring. Since R&D and innovation activities are typically uncertain, long-term, and difficult to collateralize, they are particularly sensitive to financial constraints [21]. When firms face severe financing constraints, they may be unable to sustain the complementary investments required for digital–green transformation, even if they have incentives to improve carbon performance. Therefore, financing constraints may reduce firms’ ability to convert DRTC into carbon performance gains [22,23].

Moreover, the carbon performance effect of DRTC is unlikely to be uniform across firms. Firms differ in resource endowments, complementary assets, organizational capabilities, and technological foundations, which may affect their ability to convert digital–real integration into low-carbon outcomes. Large firms usually have richer data resources, stronger managerial systems, and greater financial slack, allowing them to benefit more from DRTC. Asset-heavy firms may also obtain larger marginal benefits because their production systems provide more opportunities for energy optimization, equipment up grading, and process improvement. Therefore, firm size and asset structure are meaningful dimensions for examining heterogeneity within the relationship between DRTC and corporate carbon performance.

Against this backdrop, this study employs data from Chinese A-share listed companies covering the period 2012–2023 to investigate how DRTC influences corporate carbon performance. Specifically, this study addresses three questions. First, does DRTC improve corporate carbon performance? Second, does green innovation serve as a transmission mechanism through which DRTC affects corporate carbon performance? Third, under what innovation-resource and financial conditions is the carbon performance effect of DRTC strengthened or weakened?

This study offers three main contributions. First, it advances the literature on the determinants of corporate carbon performance by moving beyond general digital transformation and focusing specifically on DRTC. This perspective helps reveal the environmental signifi cance of the deep integration between digital technologies and physical production systems. Second, it incorporates green innovation into the analytical framework and explains the micro-level pathway through which DRTC improves corporate carbon performance. Third, this study identifies the boundary conditions of the DRTC–carbon performance relationship by examining the moderating roles of corporate R&D investment and financing constraints. This design helps explain why the effect of DRTC on carbon performance depends not only on the extent of digital–real integration but also on firms’ financial commitment to innovation and their ability to obtain external financing.

## 2. Theoretical Framework and Hypothesis Development

## 2.1. Digital–Real Technology Convergence and Corporate Carbon Performance

Corporate carbon performance provides a useful measure for evaluating the effectiveness of firms’ low-carbon operations and green development practices. Unlike indicators that focus only on absolute carbon emissions or carbon emission intensity, corporate carbon performance emphasizes the economic output generated under carbon emission constraints. It therefore captures both environmental improvement and economic efficiency in firms low-carbon transition. Recent studies also emphasize that corporate carbon performance should be assessed in a comparable and accurate manner because carbon-related outcomes are closely associated with firms’ production characteristics, operational efficiency, and environmental management practices [24].

Digital–real technology convergence (DRTC) denotes the deep integration of digital technologies with real-sector production technologies in areas such as R&D, manufacturing, supply-chain coordination, and operational management. Compared with conventional dig ital transformation, which often focuses on the adoption of digital tools in existing business processes, DRTC emphasizes the embedded interaction between digital systems and physi cal production systems. Through the integration of data, algorithms, platforms, sensors, intelligent equipment, and manufacturing processes, DRTC can reshape firms’ production modes, organizational routines, and technological capabilities. Prior research indicates that deep digital–real integration can foster disruptive innovation by reconfiguring techno logical combinations, organizational processes, and industrial application scenarios [25]. Studies related to Industry 4.0 further show that digital technologies can facilitate resource optimization and production improvements oriented toward sustainability.

Viewed through the lens of the resource-based view, firms can achieve sustained competitive advantages when they possess and effectively deploy valuable, rare, inimitable, and organizationally embedded resources [16]. Following this logic, DRTC can be regarded as a strategic resource bundle composed of digital infrastructure, data resources, intelligent algorithms, production equipment, technical knowledge, and organizational routines. This resource bundle is valuable because it helps firms identify inefficient energy use, optimize production scheduling, reduce material waste, and improve carbon management decisions. It is also difficult to imitate because its effectiveness is embedded in firm-specific production scenarios, data accumulation, managerial routines, and cross-functional coordination capa bilities. Therefore, DRTC may become an important firm-specific capability for improving low-carbon operational efficiency.

DRTC may improve corporate carbon performance through several pathways. First, by connecting digital technologies with manufacturing systems, enterprises are able to capture and analyze real-time energy-consumption data, equipment operation, and production scheduling, thereby enhancing energy efficiency and lowering carbon emissions per unit of output [26]. Second, DRTC can improve the transparency and traceability of carbon-related information throughout supply chains, which helps firms identify emission sources and coordinate emission-reduction activities with upstream and downstream partners. This is important because supply-chain relationships and interfirm asymmetries may influence the carbon emission effects of digital innovation [27]. Third, DRTC can support low-carbon product design and life-cycle management by incorporating carbon emission estimation into early-stage R&D and product design decisions, thereby reducing emissions from the source [28].

Overall, DRTC is expected to enhance corporate carbon performance by improving production efficiency, strengthening carbon information management, supporting supply-chain coordination, and enabling low-carbon product design. These mechanisms suggest that DRTC can transform digital and physical technological resources into measurable carbon performance improvements. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. Digital–real technology convergence has a positive effect on corporate carbon performance.

## 2.2. The Mediating Role of Green Innovation

Green innovation may serve as an important transformation mechanism through which firms convert DRTC into improved corporate carbon performance. This study conceptualizes green innovation as a set of technological and managerial innovation activities that seek to alleviate environmental pressures, improve resource utilization efficiency, and facilitate low-carbon development. These activities include cleaner production processes, energy-saving technologies, pollution-control technologies, environmentally friendly prod ucts, and carbon-oriented management practices [29]. Unlike corporate carbon performance, which reflects the realized efficiency outcome associated with carbon emissions, green innovation captures ex ante innovation activities and capability-building processes. Existing research indicates that green innovation can enhance firms’ environmental perfor mance, implying that innovation-driven environmental practices may ultimately improve carbon performance outcomes [30].

The mediating role of green innovation can be interpreted through the lens of dynamic capability theory. Dynamic capability theory highlights firms’ ability to integrate, develop, and reconfigure internal and external resources to adapt to changes in technology, markets, and the natural environment [17]. In the context of low-carbon transition, firms need to go beyond the adoption of digital technologies and production resources by transforming them into cleaner technological solutions, greener production processes, and low-carbon products. Following this logic, DRTC provides firms with digital infrastructure, data resources, intelligent algorithms, and production-scenario knowledge, while green innovation functions as the reconfiguration mechanism that transforms these resources into carbon performance improvements. Therefore, DRTC creates the technological and organizational conditions for green innovation, and green innovation further translates these conditions into measurable low-carbon outcomes.

DRTC can promote green innovation by improving firms’ data acquisition, knowledge integration, technological search, and collaborative innovation capabilities. By integrating digital technologies with R&D, manufacturing, supply-chain management, and market response systems, firms can more accurately identify green technological opportunities, detect high-energy-consumption links, and respond to low-carbon market requirements. A range of Industry 4.0 technologies, such as artificial intelligence, cloud computing, indus trial platforms, and intelligent equipment, can support simulation, process optimization, and real-time monitoring, thereby reducing the cost and uncertainty of green R&D [27]. In addition, digital platforms facilitate knowledge sharing across internal departments and external partners, which helps firms develop cleaner production processes, energy-saving technologies, and environmentally friendly products [31].

Green innovation, in turn, can improve corporate carbon performance through process, product, and management pathways. Through green process innovation, firms can improve high-energy-consuming and high-emission production processes by introducing cleaner technologies, enhancing energy-use efficiency, and reducing material waste. Green product innovation facilitates the development of products characterized by lower energy consumption, reduced pollution, greater recyclability, and higher environmental value over their life cycle. Green management innovation further improves carbon monitoring systems, emission-reduction target setting, environmental responsibility mechanisms, and coordinated resource allocation practices. Together, these innovation activities can lower carbon emissions per unit of output and enhance the economic efficiency of carbon resource utilization. Empirical evidence also suggests that green technology innovation contributes to carbon dioxide emission reduction [32].

Taken together, DRTC provides the technological foundation and data-driven conditions for green innovation, while green innovation transforms DRTC-related resources into cleaner processes, low-carbon products, and systematic carbon management practices. Therefore, green innovation is expected to serve as a mediating mechanism through which DRTC affects corporate carbon performance. Based on the above discussion, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. Green innovation mediates the relationship between digital–real technology convergence and corporate carbon performance.

## 2.3. The Moderating Role of Corporate R&D Investment

Corporate R&D investment refers to the financial, human, and technological resources that firms allocate to research and development activities. It reflects firms’ strategic commitment to knowledge accumulation, technological exploration, and innovation capability building. Viewed through the lens of absorptive capacity, R&D investment serves as a key antecedent that strengthens firms’ ability to identify and apply external knowledge to innovation and performance enhancement [20]. R&D also has a dual role: it directly supports technological innovation and indirectly enhances firms’ ability to learn from, assimilate, and apply external knowledge [33]. Accordingly, R&D investment can affect firms’ ability to translate external technological opportunities into operational efficiency gains and environmental performance improvements.

This study treats R&D investment as a moderator rather than a mediator because the focus is not on whether DRTC increases firms’ R&D input, but on whether firms with different R&D-based absorptive capacities differ in their ability to convert DRTC into carbon performance improvements. According to absorptive capacity theory, firms’ ability to acquire, assimilate, transform, and exploit external knowledge varies with their accumulated knowledge bases and organizational capabilities [34]. This theoretical logic is particularly relevant to DRTC because the value of digital–real integration is highly application-specific. DRTC involves the integration of digital technologies, data resources, intelligent equipment, industrial software, production systems, and organizational routines. Its value does not depend only on the availability of digital technologies, but also on whether firms can understand, adapt, and embed these technologies into real-sector production and carbon management scenarios.

Higher R&D investment enables firms to build richer technological knowledge bases, attract and retain skilled R&D personnel, and gain more experience in embedding new technologies into production processes. These conditions can help firms identify the carbon-reduction potential of DRTC more accurately. For example, firms with stronger R&D foundations may be better able to use digital technologies to diagnose energy inefficiencies, redesign production processes, develop cleaner technologies, and optimize carbon-related operational decisions. Existing studies further show that technology-push factors, including innovation capabilities and R&D activities, can promote eco-innovation and improve environmental outcomes [35]. In this sense, R&D investment strengthens the capacity of firms to convert DRTC from a technological resource bundle into practical low-carbon applications.

In addition, higher R&D investment can improve firms’ ability to transform the knowledge spillovers associated with DRTC into complementary low-carbon applications and carbon management improvements. Digital–real convergence often requires complementary innovation in production processes, equipment upgrading, data governance, algorithmic optimization, and organizational coordination. Firms with sufficient R&D investment are more capable of conducting these complementary activities, thereby deep ening the integration between digital technologies and physical production systems. This argument is consistent with evidence that absorptive capacity strengthens firms’ ability to manage and benefit from external knowledge flows [36]. Consequently, DRTC is more likely to generate improvements in corporate carbon performance when firms possess stronger absorptive capacity derived from sustained R&D investment.

By contrast, firms with relatively low R&D investment may lack the knowledge re serves, technical personnel, and experimentation capacity needed to fully exploit DRTC. Even if such firms introduce advanced digital technologies or intelligent equipment, they may only achieve superficial digital applications rather than deep integration with produc tion and carbon management systems. In this case, the potential benefits of DRTC for energy efficiency improvement, emission reduction, and low-carbon process innovation may be weakened. Therefore, insufficient R&D investment may constrain the transformation of digital–real technological advantages into improved corporate carbon performance.

Overall, R&D investment can strengthen firms’ absorptive capacity and enhance their ability to transform DRTC into low-carbon operational improvements, green technological applications, and carbon performance gains. Accordingly, this study investigates the moderating role of R&D investment in the relationship between DRTC and corporate carbon performance, while treating green innovation as a separate mediating mechanism that explains how DRTC is transformed into carbon performance. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. R&D investment strengthens the positive relationship between digital–real technology convergence and corporate carbon performance.

## 2.4. The Moderating Role of Financing Constraints

Financing constraints describe the extent to which firms encounter difficulties in accessing adequate external financial resources for investment activities. These constraints arise from capital market imperfections, such as information asymmetry, agency problems, transaction costs, and credit rationing [37,38]. As external financing is typically more costly than internal financing, firms facing financing constraints are more likely to depend on internal cash flow in their investment decisions [39]. This issue is particularly relevant to digital–real technology convergence (DRTC), because DRTC requires continuous investment in digital infrastructure, intelligent equipment, industrial software, data governance, R&D collaboration, technical personnel, and organizational adaptation. Therefore, this study treats financing constraints as a moderator because it examines how pre-existing differences in financial resource availability condition the effectiveness of DRTC, rather than whether DRTC itself changes firms’ financing constraints.

DRTC involves more than the adoption of digital tools; it represents a systemic transformation process that requires complementary investments in technologies, organizational routines, production systems, and human capital. Prior studies suggest that information technology creates value only when combined with complementary organizational and intangible investments [40]. When firms face lower financing constraints, they are more likely to have sufficient funds to invest in digital technologies, equipment upgrading, low-carbon process optimization, and related green innovation activities. Adequate financial resources also allow firms to bear the uncertainty, adjustment costs, and trial-and-error costs associated with technological convergence. As a result, firms with fewer financing constraints are better positioned to embed digital technologies into real-sector production systems and convert DRTC into energy efficiency improvements, emission-reduction practices, and carbon management capabilities.

By contrast, firms facing severe financing constraints may find it difficult to implement DRTC in depth, even when they recognize its potential value for carbon reduction. Innovation and R&D activities are especially sensitive to financing constraints because they are uncertain, knowledge-intensive, and often lack sufficient collateral value [41]. When access to capital is limited, firms may be compelled to prioritize short-term operational needs rather than long-term technological transformation. Consequently, investments in digital infrastructure, R&D projects, equipment upgrading, environmental facilities, and specialized technical personnel may be insufficient. In such cases, DRTC may remain at the level of fragmented digital application rather than deep integration with production processes and carbon management systems, weakening its potential contribution to carbon performance improvement.

Financing constraints may also limit the complementary green innovation activities through which DRTC is translated into carbon performance improvements. Green innovation and low-carbon transformation usually require continuous R&D expenditure, technological experimentation, process redesign, product development, and environmental management investment. Evidence from environmental innovation research shows that financial barriers can inhibit firms’ environmental innovation activities and reduce their ability to respond to low-carbon transformation requirements [42]. Therefore, even if DRTC provides technological opportunities, financing constraints may reduce firms’ ability to transform these opportunities into cleaner production processes, low-carbon products, and systematic carbon governance practices.

Overall, financing constraints can restrict firms’ resource allocation for DRTC and related green innovation activities. As a result, the conversion of technological conver gence into actual carbon-reduction outcomes may be weakened. Accordingly, financing constraints may attenuate the positive effect of DRTC on corporate carbon performance. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 4. Financing constraints weaken the positive effect of digital–real technology convergence on corporate carbon performance.

From the perspective of the resource-based view, DRTC can be regarded as a strategic heterogeneous resource characterized by value, rarity, inimitability, and non-substitutability.

However, heterogeneous resources do not automatically lead to performance advantages; their value depends on firms’ ability to allocate, integrate, and transform these resources effectively. Green innovation serves as this pivotal transformation mechanism, bridging the technological potential of DRTC and actual corporate carbon outcomes, and converting DRTC dividends into tangible emission reductions. This transformation process is further shaped by internal firm capabilities and external financing conditions. On the one hand, absorptive capacity theory suggests that sustained R&D investment strengthens firms ability to absorb external technological spillovers, thereby amplifying the green effects of DRTC. On the other hand, financing constraint theory indicates that capital market frictions make external financing costly. When firms face severe financing bottlenecks, even strategically valuable DRTC initiatives may lack the sustained capital required for effective deployment, thus attenuating their carbon-improvement effects. Taken together, the four hypotheses form a logically coherent chain: DRTC directly improves carbon performance (H1) and also operates indirectly through green innovation (H2), and these effects are moderated by R&D investment (H3) and financing constraints (H4).

## 3. Research Design

## 3.1. Data Sources

The empirical analysis is based on a panel sample of Chinese A-share listed manufacturing firms from 2012 to 2023. Data for calculating corporate carbon performance and the financing constraint index are obtained from the CSMAR database. Digital–real technology convergence (DRTC) data, measured using patent citation information, are sourced from the CNIPA patent database. Green innovation data, identified through green invention patents, are collected from the CNRDS database. Firm-level data, including R&D investment and control variables, are collected from the CSMAR database. To ensure data reliability and validity, several sample-screening procedures are implemented. Specifically, firms in the financial industry are excluded, as are firms designated as ST, \*ST, or PT and firms delisted during the sample period. In addition, observations with missing values in the main research variables are excluded. To alleviate the effect of extreme values, all continuous variables are winsorized at the 1% and 99% levels. Following these screening and preprocessing procedures, the final sample comprises an unbalanced panel of 20,919 firm-year observations.

## 3.3. Variable Measurement

## 3.3.1. Dependent Variable: Corporate Carbon Performance

Given the generally low level of voluntary disclosure of corporate carbon emissions across firms, this study adopts an indirect estimation approach to measure firm-level carbon emissions, which is derived from industry-level total carbon emission data. This industry-level apportionment method is supported by prior studies [43]. Based on this, corporate carbon performance (CPER) is measured as the ratio of operating revenue per 10 million RMB to carbon emissions. This indicator essentially captures the economic output generated per unit of carbon emissions [44]. In economic terms, a higher value of this measure indicates that a firm is able to generate greater operating revenue under the same level of carbon emissions, thereby reflecting superior carbon performance. The specific calculation formula is as follows:

$$
\mathrm{CPER} = \frac {\text { Corporate   Operating   Revenue } \times \text { Total   Industry   Operating   Costs }}{\text { Corporate   Operating   Costs } \times \text { Total   Industry   Carbon   Emissions } \times 1 0 0}\tag{6}
$$

Although this industry-level apportionment approach inevitably assumes homogeneous carbon emission intensity across firms within the same industry—which may obscure firm-level heterogeneity—it remains the most feasible and widely accepted proxy under China’s current corporate carbon disclosure regime, where mandatory firm-specific emission data are largely unavailable and disclosure remains predominantly voluntary (Li et al., 2024) [43]. To mitigate potential measurement bias, we control for industry-year joint fixed effects in all robustness specifications and exclude potentially misclassified outliers that may arise from ambiguous industry classifications. We acknowledge this limitation and discuss its implications extensively in the limitations subsection of the conclusion.

## 3.3.2. Independent Variable: Digital–Real Technology Convergence

Digital–real technology convergence (DRTC). This study employs a patent citationbased approach proposed by prior studies [45] to quantify firm-level DRTC. Technological convergence between digital and real sectors occurs when patents pertaining to physical and industrial technologies reference existing digital technology patents. To construct the core explanatory variable, we aggregate annual convergence events by firm and adopt the natural logarithm of one plus the aggregated number to eliminate skewness.

## 3.3.3. Mediating Variable: Green Innovation

Consistent with the prior literature, firms’ green innovation is measured by the natural logarithm of one plus the number of green patent applications [46]. Green patent applications, rather than granted patents, are employed for measurement. This is mainly because patent grants are subject to a significant time lag, as it often takes several years for an application to be approved. Compared with granted patents, application data are more timely and stable, and therefore better reflect firms’ current green innovation activities.

## 3.3.4. Moderating Variables: Corporate RD Investment and Financing Constraints

Following the prevailing specification in prior studies [47], this study measures corporate R&D investment using R&D intensity, calculated as total R&D expenditures divided by operating revenue in year t. This indicator captures the financial input dimension of firms’ R&D activities and improves comparability across firms of different sizes. A higher value indicates that a firm allocates a larger share of its operating revenue to R&D activities, reflecting stronger financial commitment to technological innovation. Since R&D intensity mainly reflects capital input, it does not fully capture other dimensions of R&D activities, such as R&D personnel input or accumulated technological knowledge. Therefore, the interpretation of this variable is limited to the financial investment dimension of corporate R&D activities.

Financing constraints are constructed based on a comprehensive index. Drawing on Jiang (2017) and subsequent relevant research [48], the FC index is introduced to gauge the magnitude of firms’ financing frictions. ## 4.3. Endogeneity Tests

Overall, the benchmark regressions support a statistically significant and positive association between DRTC and corporate carbon performance. Despite the robustness of the benchmark findings, the empirical correlation may be subject to potential endogeneity concerns. To address potential endogeneity and reinforce the robustness of the findings, the study further implements instrumental variable (IV) estimation and propensity score matching (PSM) as complementary robustness checks.

## 4.3.1. Instrumental Variable Approach

To mitigate bias arising from endogenous regressors, instrumental variable regressions are estimated and the Durbin–Wu–Hausman (DWH) test is performed to diagnose endogeneity. The DWH test reports a p-value of 0.0694, which rejects the null hypothesis of exogenous regressors at the 10% significance level and indicates the presence of endogeneity concerns. Because the empirical relationship between DRTC and corporate carbon performance may be affected by reverse causality, omitted variables, and measurement error, we follow the dynamic panel IV approach of Blundell and Bond (1998) [49] and Singh et al. (2023) [50], using the second- and third-order lags of DRTC as instruments. The identification strategy relies on the assumption that lagged DRTC values (t − 2 and t − 3) are highly correlated with current DRTC yet are plausibly orthogonal to contemporaneous shocks in carbon performance, except through the persistence of technological trajectories—this temporal precedence effectively mitigates reverse causality concerns, as current-period emissions cannot historically influence prior-period convergence decisions.

As shown in Column (2) of Table 4, the Kleibergen–Paap rk Wald F statistic reaches 35.728, substantially surpassing the 10% maximal IV critical threshold of 19.93, thereby validating strong instrument relevance and ruling out weak instrument bias. The Kleibergen– Paap LM statistic strongly rejects the null of underidentification (p = 0.0000), indicating that the chosen instruments are sufficiently informative. Furthermore, the Hansen J overidentification test fails to reject the null hypothesis $( p = 0 . 5 3 6 1 )$ , supporting the exogeneity and reliability of the chosen lagged instruments. The second-stage 2SLS estimation reveals that DRTC exerts a significantly positive influence on corporate carbon performance, with a coefficient of 0.1126 at the 1% statistical level. In line with the baseline regression results, this robust evidence shows that digital–real technology convergence remains a critical driver of corporate carbon performance after effectively alleviating endogenous estimation bias.

## 4.3.2. Propensity Score Matching

To deal with potential sample selection bias, a propensity score matching (PSM) procedure is applied. Propensity scores are obtained from a logit model in which firm financial indicators and governance characteristics are used as matching covariates. One-to-one nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.05 is implemented, and a common sup port requirement is imposed to enhance the quality of the matches. The random seed is set to 12,345 to guarantee reproducibility. After matching, the final sample consists of 3155 valid observations.

The balance test results (Table A1) show that multiple covariates exhibited significant differences before matching. After matching, the standardized bias for all covariates falls below 10%, and none of the covariates remain statistically significant at the 5% level, indicating satisfactory covariate balance between treated and control groups. The overall balancing summary statistics (Table A2) further confirm the high matching quality: the pseudo R<sup>2</sup> declines from 0.060 to 0.002, the mean bias reduces from 19.0% to 3.2%, the Rubin (2001) B-statistic decreases from 70.6 to 10.5, and the R-statistic changes from 0.79 to 0.99. All indicators satisfy the standard criteria for matching validity.

The common support test (Figure A1) reveals extensive overlap in propensity score distributions between the treatment and control groups over the main range of scores (approximately 0 to 0.6). Observations outside the common support region are automatically excluded, which validates the common support assumption.

## 5. Discussion

This study examines whether and how digital–real technology convergence (DRTC) improves corporate carbon performance. Overall, the empirical results indicate that DRTC positively influences corporate carbon performance, that green innovation functions as an important mediating mechanism, and that this effect is enhanced by R&D investment but dampened by financing constraints. The heterogeneity analysis additionally reveals that the carbon performance benefits of DRTC are more substantial for large firms and asset-heavy firms. Rather than simply affirming the environmental benefits of digitalization, these results indicate that the low-carbon impact of digital technologies depends on their deep integration with real production systems, firms’ innovation capabilities, and the availability of complementary resources.

## 5.1. Dialogue with the Prior Literature

This study advances the literature on digitalization and corporate carbon mitigation by shifting the analytical focus from generic digital transformation to DRTC. Existing research has documented that digital transformation can lower carbon emissions and enhance environmental performance [52]; yet much of this work concentrates on the adoption of digital tools, platforms, or management systems rather than on their integration with production processes. By contrast, DRTC highlights the deep integration of data, algorithms, intelligent equipment, and physical production processes. This distinction matters because firm-level carbon performance improvement often requires changes in energy monitoring, production scheduling, process optimization, and equipment operation, rather than only improvements in information processing. Therefore, this study extends prior research by showing that digitalization generates stronger carbon-related value when it is embedded in firms’ real-sector production and innovation systems.

This study also advances research on the digitalization–green innovation–carbon performance relationship. Prior studies also suggest that digital transformation can stimulate green innovation and improve firms’ carbon-related outcomes [53,54]. Building on this literature, this study shows that green innovation is a key transformation mechanism linking DRTC to carbon performance. DRTC provides data resources, intelligent analytical tools, and can enrich production-related information and scenario-specific knowledge, helping firms identify green innovation opportunities and reduce the uncertainty associated with green R&D. Green innovation then converts these technological conditions into cleaner pro cesses, low-carbon products, and carbon performance improvements. This evidence also helps clarify the conceptual distinction between green innovation and carbon performance: green innovation reflects firms’ innovation-oriented efforts and capabilities, whereas carbon performance captures the realized efficiency of carbon-related value creation.

Furthermore, the study contributes by identifying the boundary conditions under which DRTC is more or less effective in improving carbon performance. Previous work has frequently examined R&D investment and financing constraints as mechanisms through which digitalization influences firm outcomes. This study instead treats them as moderators and shows that they condition firms’ ability to realize the carbon-reduction value of DRTC. R&D investment strengthens this relationship by enhancing absorptive capacity, while financing constraints weaken it by limiting complementary investments in equipment upgrading, data infrastructure, skilled labor, and green transformation. Our findings are consistent with studies underscoring the importance of financial resources for corporate carbon neutrality and green transition [55], and further show that financial frictions can weaken the effectiveness of DRTC as a low-carbon transformation instrument.

Finally, this study responds to calls for more attention to firm heterogeneity in the environmental effects of digitalization. Existing studies suggest that firm size can shape the environmental impacts of digital transformation, as larger firms typically possess stronger resource-integration capabilities and benefit from economies of scale [56]. This study further shows that asset structure is also important. Asset-heavy firms have more physical production systems, equipment, and energy-consuming processes into which digital technologies can be embedded, creating greater scope for energy optimization and process improvement. The results therefore imply that the benefits of digital–green transformation are unevenly distributed and depend on firms’ resource endowments and production-system characteristics.

## 5.2. Theoretical Contributions

This study offers three theoretical contributions. First, it enriches the literature on corporate carbon performance by conceptualizing DRTC as a distinct driver of firm-level low-carbon transformation. Unlike conventional digital transformation, DRTC highlights the deep coupling between digital technologies and real-world production systems. This perspective helps explain why digitalization may generate heterogeneous carbon outcomes across firms and why isolated digital adoption may be insufficient to produce substantial carbon performance improvements.

Second, this study enriches mechanism research on digitalization and carbon reduction by identifying green innovation as a key mediating pathway. By distinguishing green innovation from carbon performance, this study clarifies how DRTC resources are converted into low-carbon outcomes. It contributes to the green innovation literature by showing that green innovation is not merely an environmental strategy, but also a capability-building process that converts DRTC into carbon efficiency and low-carbon value creation.

Third, this study advances the boundary-condition perspective of digital–green transformation. By examining R&D investment and financing constraints as moderating factors, the study demonstrates that the influence of DRTC on carbon performance hinges on firms internal absorptive capacity and their external financial resource conditions. This provides a more contingent understanding of the digitalization–carbon performance relationship and explains why the same level of DRTC may generate different carbon outcomes across firms.

## 6. Conclusions and Policy Implications

## 6.1. Conclusions

Building on the resource-based view, absorptive capacity theory, and financing constraint theory, this study examines the relationship between DRTC and corporate carbon performance using data on Chinese A-share listed manufacturing firms over the period 2012–2023. A two-way fixed-effects framework is applied to identify the main effect as well as the mediating mechanisms and moderating factors. The core results can be summarized as follows.

First, DRTC significantly enhances corporate carbon performance, and this robust positive relationship withstands a battery of sensitivity tests. Second, green innovation serves as an essential mediating channel linking DRTC to improvements in carbon performance.

Third, R&D investment amplifies the positive impact of DRTC on carbon performance, while financing constraints attenuate this effect. Fourth, the carbon performance dividends of DRTC are more pronounced among large-sized and capital-intensive, asset-heavy firms.

## 6.2. Policy Implications

First, efforts should be made to firmly advance digital–real technology convergence and fully leverage its whole-process carbon reduction effects. Firms should recognize DRTC as an important pathway for low-carbon development, as it can fundamentally help reduce corporate carbon emissions. In the product R&D stage, digital technologies can be used to simulate product carbon emissions in advance. This helps transform the advantages of digital technologies into carbon reduction outcomes through full-process technological integration. Second, building on DRTC, firms should promote green innovation. They need to integrate digital–real technologies with green innovation and develop green devel opment models suited to their own resource endowments and strategic contexts. Third, firms should appropriately increase R&D investment to enhance the effectiveness of DRTC. Particular attention should be paid to investment in core convergence technologies and green environmental technologies. Ongoing R&D investment enables firms to accumulate technological knowledge and enhances their ability to absorb and utilize spillovers generated by DRTC. Fourth, coordinated efforts from multiple stakeholders are needed to ease firms’ financing constraints. Both DRTC and green innovation require substantial financial investment. Such activities typically involve long payback horizons and considerable risk, making it challenging for firms to depend exclusively on internal funding. Firms should improve their financial management systems and enhance information disclosure to banks and investors to secure greater financial support. Fifth, large firms should use their financial and technological strengths to lead the deep implementation of DRTC and actively share technologies and experience. Asset-heavy firms should further deepen DRTC, summarize replicable successful practices, and transfer technologies and mature models to asset-light firms. This will promote industry-wide low-carbon transformation.

## 6.3. Limitations and Implicationsfor Future Research

While this research offers a systematic empirical examination of the carbon performance implications arising from digital–real technology convergence, several limitations remain and offer avenues for future research.

First, the measurement accuracy of corporate carbon performance is constrained. This study indirectly estimates firm-level emissions from industry-level data, assuming homogeneous emission intensity within industries, which may obscure firm-level heterogeneity. Future research could integrate voluntary disclosure and carbon trading data to construct a more comprehensive, firm-specific carbon performance indicator. Second, the measurement of DRTC is relatively narrow, capturing only technological convergence through patent citations while neglecting organizational, managerial, and business model dimensions. Future research could develop a multi-dimensional measurement system incorporating textual analysis of annual reports and digitalization investment data. Third, the mediating mechanism is only partially explored because the analysis relies on an aggregate measure of green innovation and does not differentiate between green-process and green-product innovation. Future work could examine other mechanisms such as energy efficiency optimization and supply-chain collaboration. Fourth, the analysis of boundary conditions has certain limitations. On the one hand, the measurement of R&D investment relies on R&D intensity, which primarily reflects the capital-input dimension of innovation activities. R&D intensity does not fully capture R&D personnel input, innovation outputs, or firms’ accumulated technological knowledge base. Accordingly, the moderating effect of R&D investment should be interpreted as reflecting the role of financial commitment to R&D activities rather than firms’ overall innovation resources or technological capabilities. Future studies could incorporate multidimensional innovation indicators, such as R&D personnel intensity, patent quality, green patent citations, or knowledge stock, to provide a more comprehensive assessment. On the other hand, the moderators examined are restricted to firm-internal factors, without considering external institutional conditions. Future research could incorporate environmental regulation intensity, regional digital infrastructure, and executives’ green cognition as additional moderators. Fifth, the sample is limited to Chinese A-share listed manufacturing firms during 2012–2023, and the heterogeneity analysis focuses only on firm size and asset structure. Future research could expand the sample to non-listed enterprises and high-emission industries, and examine heterogeneity across ownership types, regions, and industry-level emission intensity. Sixth, the strength of causal inference in this study remains constrained. Although we have alleviated some endogeneity concerns through instrumental variable estimation, the empir ical design cannot entirely rule out all potential omitted variable biases. Accordingly, the results—especially those related to the mediation and heterogeneity analyses—should be viewed as robust correlational evidence rather than definitive causal claims. Future research could enhance causal identification by exploiting quasi-natural experiments, including digital infrastructure pilot programs or carbon emissions trading pilots.
